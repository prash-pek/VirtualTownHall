The supporters of the “Your 2 cents PDX” ballot measure start with a reasonable premise: Portlanders are frustrated with their city government and have lost trust in their elected leaders. Something needs to change.
However, the solution they offer — creating a “participatory budgeting” program in which the city sets aside the equivalent of 2% of the general fund for projects selected by Portlanders — would only make matters worse. The measure would enshrine in the city charter a program larger than New York City’s on a per-capita basis. In its first year, it would claim $16.4 million in scarce funds, even though Portland just reduced community center hours, closed homeless shelters, raided savings and laid off 100 people to bridge a $170 million budget gap.
And rather than true “budgeting,” which requires balancing multiple priorities and obligations with limited resources, the measure essentially would create another big grant program with no direct accountability for which projects are funded.
Portlanders already frustrated with high taxes and low levels of service should resist the calls to create another funding silo and vote “no” on Measure 26-267.
Certainly, participatory budgeting programs aren’t new, with various versions in place overseas as well as in the U.S. Supporters contend that such programs can help improve public engagement and trust because people can see the results of getting involved and voting on projects that come to fruition. The measure’s advocates also say that creating an annual program will help the city build the infrastructure for connecting with Portlanders of all backgrounds, rather than just the lobbyists or subset of residents who are active in politics.
But Portland’s proposal takes it to a new level. The estimated $16.4 million for the program’s first year is far larger than the amounts dedicated by most other U.S. cities with an annual participatory budgeting program, including Los Angeles ( $8.5 million), Boston ($2.2 million) and Denver ($2 million). And on a per capita basis, that amount — $25-a-person — dwarfs the $3.40 a person that New York City sets aside for its two participatory budgeting programs. Even days after an endorsement interview, supporters could not name a single U.S. city that devotes more per capita annually than this program would.
Advocates argue the amount is “a rounding error” in Portland’s overall $8.5 billion budget, glossing over the fact that 90% of Portland’s revenue is restricted for use in specific areas, such as making debt payments or fulfilling levy requirements. Instead, the city would likely need to tap the flexible dollars in the roughly $800 million general fund which largely pays for police, fire, parks and transportation.

After a bruising budget battle in which city councilors scrapped over every last dollar, the prospect of diverting $16 million is alarming — especially, as the city stares down yet another budget shortfall. “We are, frankly, in crisis response,” Mayor Keith Wilson said during the endorsement interview. A recurring allocation for the participatory budgeting program would inevitably translate into fewer police officers, less parks maintenance and more crumbling streets.
Despite supporters’ arguments that participatory budgeting programs help engage the general public in government, only a tiny percentage of residents actually vote. It’s unclear how Portland would structure its program, who could vote, or how much it would cost to administer, since the measure leaves that to the city to figure out. But in Seattle — which still has not delivered on projects selected in 2023 as part of its one-time participatory budgeting investment — only 4,300 people, ages 15 and older, voted in the city of 785,000. Metro, which piloted a program as part of a bond measure, received only 3,164 votes from people ages 11 and older in the Portland metro area to decide how to spend $3 million.
That’s a small, unrepresentative, unelected group making decisions on how millions of tax dollars are spent. While people may disagree with the decisions made by their elected leaders, they have recourse to communicate their opposition or to vote against them.
But even more important, there’s no assurances that the same activists and connected community members who already stack Council chambers won’t similarly dominate this process as well. Those Portlanders who feel unheard in the regular budgeting process could very well be left out in this program as well.
And finally, the whole idea behind changing Portland’s city charter two years ago was to create a more representative government. We supersized our city council, creating four new districts with three councilors in each. We changed the way we vote to improve the likelihood that minority viewpoints are represented on the council. It is the job of the 12 city councilors to engage with their constituents so that they can make informed budgeting decisions. Instead, strangely, six of the 12 councilors are backing the measure.
Portland voters should avoid making that same mistake. Reject this new unfunded, unaccountable grant program that siphons money from public services by voting “no” on Measure 26-267.
-The Oregonian/OregonLive Editorial Board
