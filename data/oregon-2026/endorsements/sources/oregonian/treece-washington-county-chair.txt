Sooner or later, Washington County voters will have a new chair helming the county’s board of commissioners, and the change can’t come quickly enough.
Outgoing Chair Kathryn Harrington’s offensive gesture to Commissioner Nafisa Fai last week — the latest of several incidents in which Harrington has acted rudely and unprofessionally — has spurred widespread calls for her resignation. Harrington, who said her behavior reflected ongoing mental health struggles, announced only that she is handing over chair responsibilities to other commissioners, albeit without reducing her $145,840-a-year paycheck, as The Oregonian/OregonLive’s Austin De Dios reported. Her refusal to step down only sharpens the contrast with Fai, who has handled the entire matter with remarkable grace.
Voters can’t do much to dislodge an unwilling Harrington before her term ends Jan. 3. But in the November election, they can look to the future and choose the candidate who will best steer the county through its challenges and restore stability. While Fai offers a strong argument, fellow Washington County Commissioner Pam Treece emerges as the best candidate with the skills and experience to do so.
We endorsed Treece, 75, over her two opponents in the May election and she continues to make the case for her election. A former executive director of the Westside Economic Alliance and former PacifiCorp executive, the two-term District 2 commissioner thoroughly understands the full span of the county’s responsibilities, supplementing a big picture perspective with on-the-ground details. She highlights growing unaffordability as the top issue for the board to address and names ways to spur economic activity that can translate into higher-paying jobs and more revenue for governmental services.
Treece’s ability to identify solutions that are grounded in facts, cognizant of limited resources and informed by public sentiment also sets her apart. She demonstrated that threading of the needle in a recent board work session as commissioners considered a request by Fai to adopt a moratorium on data center construction for unincorporated Washington County.
It’s safe to say that no Oregon politician wanting to be elected is cheerleading for data centers at this point. Massive energy and water usage by giant data centers for Google, Facebook and similar companies, as well as the tax breaks many received, have generated intense backlash from the public as families see their own utility bills surge. Hillsboro, an epicenter for data centers in both Washington County and the state as a whole, has adopted a moratorium on new data centers, giving officials 120 days to develop the guardrails for what they will allow and require.
But the fear of data center development hasn’t always matched the facts. And unincorporated Washington County — the area over which the county board of commissioners has land use authority — has only eight parcels of land eligible to house data centers, with only three being larger than 40 acres, according to county staff. Most important, all eight sites are already developed and in use by the owners. As another commissioner noted, the county already has a “de facto” moratorium in place.

Rather than reassign staff from other priorities to research requirements for a regulatory, environmental and taxation structure for a highly unlikely scenario, Treece championed a resolution and order that won’t preclude a moratorium, but articulates the community’s concerns. She also added her support to Fai’s call to establish a task force on data centers, which the two are expected to lead together.
Fai, a former public health official, former refugee and Washington County’s first Black commissioner, brings a needed perspective to the board. Her emphasis on ensuring broader access to services for small businesses and immigrant communities shows her deep commitment to ensuring prosperity for those who have historically been left behind. And she has pushed the board to be more proactive addressing the community’s concerns on data centers.
She also shown considerable composure since Harrington raised her middle finger at Fai in the middle of a work session last week as the other commissioners were not looking.
But she does not show that same command of county operations and deliberate decision-making as Treece and her push for a moratorium on data centers reflected more populist reaction than strategic leadership. She offered only broad concepts for the economic development solutions the county can tap to ease the affordability crisis. With another two years before her current term representing District 1 expires, she can continue contributing to the board while deepening her expertise.
The upcoming election should signal to Harrington that it’s time to resign, as opposed to waiting until the end of her term. Resigning would help limit the distraction to the county as well as help preserve the legacy of her long career in public service dating back to her days as a Metro councilor. And stepping down would give her the time she deserves to address her mental health needs.
Regardless of the distraction, however, voters’ choice is clear — they should elect Treece to put the county and the board of commissioners back on course.
-The Oregonian/OregonLive Editorial Board
