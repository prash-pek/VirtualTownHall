The three candidates running for Multnomah County chair all offer their own prescriptions for fixing the dysfunction that residents know too well. One candidate seeks to completely overhaul operations and rebuild the county from the ground up, while a second candidate would largely make adjustments within the current structure.
But voters tired of waiting for real progress on the county’s crises should turn to the person who has already demonstrated the skill to lead struggling, cash-strapped organizations to stability — Julia Brim-Edwards. A county commissioner since 2023, former Portland Public Schools board member and former Nike executive, Brim-Edwards has consistently proven her commitment to accountability and system improvement to serve the public. Pragmatic and effective, she is Multnomah County’s best choice — and best chance — for righting the ship. Voters should rank Brim-Edwards as their number one candidate for chair.
Brim-Edwards, 65, has already had an impact as a county commissioner, despite the considerable power vested by charter in the board chair, Jessica Vega Pederson. She has championed the building of a new sobering center — a huge need since the previous one closed in 2019 amid soaring addiction rates. While previous efforts to replace it collapsed, Brim-Edwards has shepherded the project through funding and political challenges. The center, which will also house the county’s deflection program for drug users and provide withdrawal management treatment, is expected to open in 2027.
She has also helped find money for critical needs that faced cuts in Vega Pederson’s proposed budgets. Earlier this year, she and Commissioner Vince Jones-Dixon teamed up to reverse a $731,000 cut for homeless services in Gresham — one of the five east county cities often neglected by the county and whose mayors, along with Portland Mayor Keith Wilson, have all endorsed Brim-Edwards. She also worked with Jones-Dixon and Commissioner Meghan Moyer to restore funding for three positions in the district attorney’s office unit that handles high-impact property and retail theft cases, overcoming opposition from Vega Pederson and Commissioner Shannon Singleton.
If elected, Brim-Edwards would seek to revamp the county, though not with the wrecking ball that candidate Sharon Meieran would tee up. Rather, Brim-Edwards would set up a central command system to better provide comprehensive services for those who are homeless, breaking down some of the barriers that have needlessly complicated that goal. She also would seek a performance audit of the county’s homeless service contractors to assess their effectiveness. That focus on ensuring public dollars are delivering what the public expects has been a hallmark of her career in elected office.
Brim-Edwards has already shown she can bring order to chaos. In 2017, she rejoined the school board for Portland Public Schools (after previously serving from 2001-2005), as the district was reeling. Its longtime superintendent had been ousted over the district’s failure to address lead in drinking fountains, the board had just botched an effort to replace her and both staff morale and public trust were at crisis-level lows.
Brim-Edwards took over as board chair and quickly launched a new search that landed Guadalupe Guerrero and helped stabilize a district in distress. She spearheaded the creation of a new professional conduct policy after a 2017 Oregonian investigation revealed the district had failed for several years to heed students’ allegations about sexual advances and other misconduct by a teacher, Mitchell Whitehurst. And she pushed for the creation of an internal performance auditor to independently evaluate the effectiveness of district programs.
Certainly, she’s ruffled feathers. A critical letter from some of her former PPS board colleagues argues that she did not collaborate well, failed to lead and was unable to make difficult decisions. But her record contradicts the criticisms. PPS in 2017 was an unmitigated mess, and Brim-Edwards’ decisive leadership was lauded then by some of the same board members who signed the letter.

More recently, she has collaborated with each of her fellow commissioners to pass new policies and adjust budget priorities. And while some may object to the detailed questions she often asks of staff, that is a foundational element of oversight and accountability. In fact, the current PPS board is now embroiled in controversy over a contract that received painfully little scrutiny.
While Multnomah County voters can rank multiple candidates for a position in order of preference due to the county’s adoption of ranked-choice voting, we are recommending only Brim-Edwards. Her two opponents, however, make solid cases for their candidacies as well.
Singleton, 49, brings a wealth of experience in housing and homelessness to the county, and has been a valuable addition since being elected to the board in 2024. A former housing policy advisor to former Gov. Kate Brown who briefly served as the county’s homeless services department director under former Chair Deborah Kafoury, Singleton understands the landscape and would use that knowledge to help coordinate the county’s different departments into a more cohesive response.
But while she has distinguished herself from Vega Pederson in some ways, we fear she would represent a continuation of the Kafoury-Vega Pederson approach that has failed to adequately address the public safety and community impacts of our escalating homelessness crisis. While she said it is the city’s responsibility to set its rules for managing public spaces, she has in the past criticized plans to enforce Portland’s anti-camping ordinance.
And Singleton was a reliable vote for Vega Pederson in opposing public safety investments backed by Brim-Edwards, Jones-Dixon and Moyer last year (such as restoring money for three prosecutors handling domestic violence, civil commitment and treatment court cases) as well as this year.
Meieran, 62, served two terms as a Multnomah County commissioner from 2017-2025. An emergency room physician and lawyer, she became a relentless critic of both Kafoury and Vega Pederson, often highlighting the lack of cohesion in the county’s homelessness and behavioral health responses.
She is offering the most radical solution — essentially rebuilding the county with entirely new budgeting, a new organizational structure and new strategies. But the solution she has been advertising is essentially a plan to develop a plan, laying out big goals, overarching principles and timelines. It would be an enormous lift for any entity, much less a $4 billion operation that must at the same time continue providing essential services amid budget cuts and a reduced staff.
Meieran also lacks the track record of leading organizational change and contributed to a significant decline in collegiality in her final years at the county. We have long appreciated her courage to call out problems, but we do not see the organizational and logistical skills needed to lead such a major overhaul.
Brim-Edwards has scooped up a trove of endorsements of business, labor, mayors, community leaders and many more, for good reason. Multnomah County voters should show their support as well and elect her as the next chair.
-The Oregonian/OregonLive Editorial Board
