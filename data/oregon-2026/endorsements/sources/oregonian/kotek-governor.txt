As the Democratic incumbent in a state firmly under Democratic control, Gov. Tina Kotek should be cruising to reelection. The fact that polls show a dead heat between her and Republican challenger Sen. Christine Drazan — despite President Donald Trump’s profound unpopularity — tells the story of just how frustrated Oregonians are.
Kotek, 60, is vulnerable for a reason — or several. Oregon’s economy has continued to weaken, with higher unemployment and anemic growth; homelessness is rising, contrary to the nationwide decline; and K-12 student performance continues to rank among the worst in the country. Despite hundreds of millions of dollars of investment, Oregonians still can’t access the behavioral health services they need. And even on Kotek’s signature issue of housing, the state has shown only middling progress. Oregon has never come close to meeting the housing goals she announced on her inauguration day.
We share Oregonians’ frustration, both with Kotek and our choices.
Drazan, a former House minority leader and 2022 Republican nominee for governor, offers solid policy ideas for Oregon’s crises. But we are unconvinced she’s the leader to guide Oregon through the turmoil, including the threats brought by the Trump Administration. Kotek’s best moments as governor have been in her defense of the state and advocacy for Oregon ideals, while Drazan has been silent or, worse, added fuel to the fire. A third candidate, Pacific Green nominee Brett Smith, is not a viable option.
With deep ambivalence, we endorse Kotek for a second term.
It’s worth noting that we felt similarly conflicted when we endorsed Kotek in 2022 over Drazan and former senator Betsy Johnson, an independent. Then, as now, there were plenty of moments that showed Kotek at her best and at her worst.
Kotek’s term started off strong with action on housing policy and homelessness. The state added hundreds of shelter beds, provided dollars for rent assistance and homeless youth and set a statewide approach to setting housing goals for communities. The state has also established new affordable housing and construction funds and created a process for limited expansions of the urban growth boundary to accommodate housing.
Kotek also targeted longstanding educational weaknesses by prioritizing early literacy and championing a bill allowing the state to intervene in school districts showing poor outcomes.
A high point was her leadership during Trump’s crusade in fall 2025 to dispatch the National Guard to Portland, presumably to quell immigration enforcement protests that had died down weeks earlier. Her coolheaded and collaborative approach with other state and local officials helped keep a lid on the tinderbox of fury over Immigration and Customs Enforcement abuses. It cannot be overstated how difficult a challenge that was with Trump, administration officials and agitators of all political stripes intent on lighting a fuse.

But other actions have left us scratching our heads, such as her abrupt reversal on tolling, upending transportation funding plans for capital projects. One of her surprise executive orders, a 2024 mandate for contractors on state projects to adopt union labor agreements, has been overturned by a judge. And she enthusiastically signed a bill opposed by cities, counties and school districts around the state that allows workers to collect unemployment benefits while on strike. The policy — a gift for labor unions and unique to Oregon — hits public agencies especially hard, because they must cover the cost of such benefits, as Portland Community College did this year.
This transactional approach to governance, in which Kotek tries to satisfy a constituency here and cater to donors there, leaves Oregonians confused over her vision for the state — and whether she has one.
Her insistence on letting her wife, Aimee Kotek Wilson, “volunteer” on behavioral health policy caused significant conflicts that prompted top-level staffers to resign, as news accounts reported, hurting Kotek’s ability to push through her agenda. Last year’s failure to pass new revenue to fund the Oregon Department of Transportation speaks to her decline in influence, yet Kotek’s campaign said her wife would continue in the role.
All that has created an opening for Drazan, 54.
The Canby legislator has put affordability front and center in her platform with a goal to change regulations and policies that drive up housing, energy, food and other costs. To boost housing production, she shares some of Kotek’s same ideas, such as faster permitting and development timelines, but rightly points out that rent control and inclusionary zoning mandates in urban areas are stifling needed development.
She would also push for a comprehensive modernization of land use laws rather than “adding bureaucracy to bureaucracy” as Kotek has, she said.
Her education platform includes a proposal to add instructional time for students, emphasize early literacy by ensuring students can read before they can advance past third grade and strengthen funding for students with disabilities. To increase funding, she would seek to redirect the portion of the corporate activity tax that now goes to education-related grants and backfill those programs as needed from the General Fund — a controversial conversation but one that could prompt new funding strategies.
Although she adheres to standard Republican values, she said she would not seek to ban abortion and would enforce Oregon’s sanctuary law. And Drazan has shown more independence than other Republicans. She has never denied that Joe Biden was fairly elected president in 2020 and she led her caucus in expelling a Republican legislator who let rioters into the state Capitol in December 2020.
But that doesn’t mean she hasn’t been overly partisan and her role leading a walkout of House Republicans in 2020 won’t help her steer policy through the Democratic legislative majority.
Like many Republicans, she has failed to speak up against the chaos that the Trump Administration has unleashed, including widespread immigration raids that sweep up citizens and noncitizens alike, many with no criminal record. Trump’s push to slash federal aid and impose new requirements will directly hurt Oregonians dependent on food stamps and Medicaid. His campaign to break vote-by-mail is built on conspiratorial nonsense. And he has regularly brutalized the economy, the Constitution and basic standards of human decency with his tariffs, war in Iran, campaigns of fear, fabrications of executive authority and now, a plan to livestream an execution by firing squad. Trump’s actions have diminished us a country, as citizens and as humans.
And when Trump threatened to send the National Guard to Portland in 2025, Drazan did not stand up to him, instead chastising the governor and Portland mayor for not stopping “violent mobs and domestic terrorists” outside the ICE facility. She may argue that she did not “technically” support the move, but that is beside the point. As we wrote at the time, the moment cried out for statesmanship — a leader to calm the waters. Drazan was not then, and we fear, will not be that leader.
There are lessons for everyone. For Republicans — the failure to stand up to lawless and unethical behavior by party leaders carries consequences down the line. For Democrats — elected office is not a birthright for Democrats. The failure to challenge Kotek, as well as Kotek’s own failure to govern with courage, could result in Republican control. And for Oregonians — our closed-primary system, which elevates candidates who appeal to the most partisan voters does not serve Oregonians’ interests.
But those lessons shouldn’t distract from the election at hand. Oregonians should vote for Kotek. But may this election be a wake-up to the establishment to take nothing for granted anymore.
-The Oregonian/OregonLive Editorial Board
