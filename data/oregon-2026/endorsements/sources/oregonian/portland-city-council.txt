It’s too early to pass judgment on Portland’s new form of government, but nearly two years in, Portlanders have yet to get the government they need.
Putting city operations under an administrator and supersizing to a 12-member city council was supposed to provide Portland with a more professional, representative and functional government. Instead, Portlanders have had a front-row seat to an increasingly demoralizing scene: Battles between city councilors and the administration over authority and roles; growing partisanship between councilors affiliated with the Democratic Socialists of America and the independent progressives on a body meant to be nonpartisan; and ugly squabbling that leads to 6-6 deadlocks, damaged relationships and a public frustrated by Council’s paralysis.
Meanwhile, housing construction in Portland has stalled amid local and national headwinds. An exodus from downtown office buildings in recent years has led to less tax revenue and more budget cuts for the city. Hundreds of affordable housing units sit empty while thousands of people live on the streets, and families are struggling amid rising taxes, fees and household costs. These urgent realities are why so many residents were exasperated by the many days spent on Councilor Mitch Green’s ordinance to ban sales of foie gras by roughly 12 Portland restaurants and markets.
Since city councilors are stuck in stalemate, Portlanders must force a change. In this November’s election, voters living in Districts 3 and 4 have the opportunity to break the logjam. With all six of those Council seats on the ballot — three of which are held by DSA members — voters can change the composition, swap out at least one DSA councilor for an independent thinker and empower those with the experience, judgment and commitment to Portlanders to lead the council.
Certainly, the legislative body should reflect Portland’s liberal make-up, including those represented by the DSA. But with four DSA councilors, the chapter — which claims 3,000 members in Portland — wields outsized influence, particularly considering the group explicitly demands that endorsed councilors coordinate their work with the DSA chapter’s priorities. Two other non-DSA councilors routinely vote with the four, leading to a bloc mentality that discourages robust debate of a proposal’s merits or collaboration to make a flawed policy better.

Our voting recommendations for City Council reflect a line-up of those we believe will best move Portland forward and squarely represent everyday Portlanders’ values. We’ve also provided our rationale for ousting incumbents — not an easy task under Portland’s new system which requires that a candidate capture only 25% plus one of the votes in their district to win. But we urge voters to think about what they want to see from their city councilors — not just priorities and accomplishments, but also their approach to the work and leadership among their peers.
Although voters can rank up to six candidates, we have endorsed only three candidates in each district. Voters should rank only those whom they support to take the $133,203-a-year job.
DISTRICT 3
District 3 spans most of inner Southeast Portland to Southeast 82nd Avenue and includes a portion of Northeast Portland. The incumbents are Steve Novick, Angelita Morillo and Tiffany Koyama Lane. We endorse challenger Kellie Torres, Novick and Morillo in that order.
Kellie Torres
Torres, 56, isn’t fazed by chaos or the lack of a roadmap. Instead, her work both inside and outside of Portland city government shows she brings the ingenuity and work ethic to find solutions for unsolved problems.
Torres previously served as City Councilor Dan Ryan’s chief of staff from 2020 to 2024 where she led the charge on setting up several “Safe Rest Villages” — alternative, tiny-home-like shelters to house the homeless and connect them to services. While similar sites have since become a well-regarded strategy for helping move people from the streets to permanent housing, the villages were controversial at the time. But Torres capably handled the logistics of building the villages, navigating political opposition and assuaging neighbors’ concerns to get them open.
Now, overseeing external affairs and partnerships in Portland Parks and Recreation, she works with community members, businesses and others to support parks and retain services without having to ask taxpayers to pay more. Her ability to navigate uncharted territory — siting a homeless village, working to reopen the Laurelhurst dance studio or helping streamline the city’s notoriously broken permit system — demonstrates the constructive cooperation needed on City Council to fix big problems. Her focus on affordability, economic vitality and improving delivery of basic public services shows her priorities line up with those of Portlanders who want this city to thrive. District 3 voters should make Torres their number one pick to help make it happen.
Steve Novick
Novick, 63, has proven his value to the City Council time and again. A former city commissioner from 2013 to 2017 and former state justice department attorney fighting environmental rollbacks, Novick has long channeled Portland’s progressive values into durable policy.
Whether or not you agree with him — and many times, we have not — Novick brings a wealth of ideas for any problem, from how to ease the impact of parks budget cuts to how a city investment on a Moda Center renovation can be repaid over time. His ideas may not pan out, but his willingness to help solve a problem, challenge sacred cows and, most important, revise solutions based on critical feedback help explain why he is trusted by a wide range of groups.
He also recognizes the bigger picture of what Portland needs, including improved public safety, a healthy economy and fiscal restraint. During the budget season earlier this year, he relentlessly argued for restoring money for critical police training and sought to cut councilors’ generous office budgets to help stave off layoffs elsewhere.
While he has, at times, voted with the DSA-led caucus, he has been a voice of reason in arguing against some of their rash or reckless actions. He led a successful effort to restore Portland Children’s Levy grants for dozens of nonprofits after a reckless decision driven by the DSA bloc left them scrambling for funding. His independence and ability to consider a proposal on its merits, not its provenance, is exactly what councilors should bring. We recommend ranking Novick second, expecting that his strong popularity and name recognition will carry him easily into a seat.
Angelita Morillo
We debated not recommending a third candidate. While both Morillo, 30, and Koyama Lane, 40, have impressed us at times, the two DSA-backed councilors also have added to the chaos and often approached policymaking as a take-it-or-leave-it equation rather than engage in genuine compromise.
But when we made our endorsements in 2024, we wrote: “the legitimacy of the next City Council depends on having a mix of voices who represent at least some of the diversity of interests in Portland. And while we may not support particular stances, differing views must inform the body’s decision-making to serve all Portland’s residents.”
We strongly believe that. And of the DSA councilors and their allies on the City Council, no one is more effective in representing those views than Morillo.
She has passed several ordinances, such as a ban on landlords’ use of AI in setting rent, has successfully shepherded budget amendments through tense discussions and makes compelling arguments from the dais. She is clearly politically talented and a force to reckon with.
Our endorsement, however, comes with deep reservations about her conduct and impact on the Council. She routinely lashes out at colleagues, accuses them of acting in bad faith and shows contempt for those who don’t share her views. While many decried her remark to Councilor Loretta Smith that Mayor Keith Wilson is Smith’s “daddy,” she has made many other comments that have contributed to a toxic environment at City Council.
Even when challenged about her behavior, she seeks to justify it. When asked by the editorial board about an ageist remark she made during a January meeting to elect a new council president, she claimed her comment came only after her colleagues were “making demeaning comments about me being a little Tik Tok girl.” She then argued that we ignored her colleagues’ conduct while chastising her.
A review of the video from that January meeting, however, shows no such remark was made in the entire discussion leading up to Morillo’s comment. When asked about it in a follow-up email, she did not directly address the discrepancy, but again complained of a double standard.
We would encourage Morillo to realize that she’s undermining her own tremendous potential with her insults and excuses. Skilled advocates can fight for their beliefs without stooping to personal attacks that drive away allies as well as those they need to persuade. It’s not sexist or ageist to expect councilors to act professionally — it’s a minimum requirement for the job Morillo is seeking.
Other candidates
Koyama Lane did not make our list. We appreciate her traffic safety advocacy, her work keeping councilors united in the face of Trump Administration actions and that she doesn’t regularly engage in the decline in decorum. But she has been little more than a rubberstamp for DSA bloc priorities, offers little in policy debates and has fanned the flames — sometimes with misinformation — on incendiary topics, such as the Moda Center renovation and Portland Public Schools’ consolidation plans.
Among the other 17 candidates, we interviewed communications consultant Tom Sollitt, 41, and physical therapist Esther Léon, 33. Sollitt, especially, stood out for his diligence in researching the issues facing the city, reaching out to community members and helping broker solutions. But neither Sollitt nor Léon have enough relevant experience to continue the work of building this fledgling government.
District 4
The three incumbent councilors for this district, which includes downtown, Portland’s westside and a portion of inner Southeast are Eric Zimmerman, Olivia Clark and Mitch Green. We endorse Zimmerman, Clark and police officer Eli Arnold in that order.
Eric Zimmerman
Zimmerman, 41, is the councilor in the room who best demonstrates what a functional Council could look like.
He injects a high level of professionalism in the City Council, informed by his prior years working in local government, as a mayoral staffer, deputy city manager and chief of staff for two county commissioners. His command of how government should work informs many of the proposals he brings to strengthen the new system, such as a pilot project to increase transparency of the city’s parks budget. And his understanding of the city’s financial situation and the broader economy distinguishes him from most of the other councilors.
That expertise has helped him pass governmental fixes as well as broad-impact legislation, such as a business tax cut that provides relief for thousands of small businesses at little cost to the General Fund. He has shepherded the passage of a fee waiver on housing development and pushed for streamlined design reviews to reduce the costs and delays that discourage home building. And he is currently working on legislation to prevent retailers from using surveillance technology to alter the price that one shopper may pay versus another.
Notably, he has been a voice of pragmatism on a city council that sometimes lacks real-world awareness. His questioning and comments identifying weaknesses in some of his fellow councilors’ proposals have earned him the ire of the DSA chapter, but voters shouldn’t be misled. Zimmerman has been one of the councilors trying to set the government up for success. District 4 voters should vote him first on their ballot to continue that progress.
Olivia Clark
After her election to the Council two years ago, Clark, 72, went to work on one of Portland’s least glamorous and most urgent issues — Portland’s multibillion-dollar infrastructural time bomb. While previous city leaders were well aware of Portland’s deteriorating streets, aging pipes and neglected facilities, too little has been done to confront the problem. This is the intractable kind of mess that Clark, a former policy analyst and legislative director, willingly took on.
She led passage of a resolution directing the city to develop a “citywide asset management strategy” to catalogue the needs, develop a strategy and propose a funding solution. But she also spearheaded the creation of a new Transportation Utility Fee to help fund necessary maintenance and upgrades of our road system. While residents likely won’t welcome the new monthly fee on their utility bills, the obligation is unavoidable if we want to have decent roads, maintained streetlights and usable sidewalks. Even with this new fee, the city will mostly be reducing further erosion as opposed to revamping the system. City leaders must press for ways to reduce costs on Portlanders, but this was a necessary move to protect our roads — a priority that voters often cite.
Her actions show two things: Clark’s willingness to dive into Portland’s thorniest problems that others would sidestep and her courage to advance a solution that may be highly unpopular.
District 4 should show their support for this essential work and rank Clark second on their ballot.
Eli Arnold
Arnold, 46, was on our endorsement list in 2024, though he narrowly lost the final seat in the district to Zimmerman. We are once again endorsing him, ranking him third.
The Portland Police Bike Squad officer helps people embroiled in our homelessness, drug addiction and mental health crises on a daily basis. Considering the energy spent each budget cycle arguing over cuts to the police bureau, it would make sense to have at the table the informed perspective of someone who knows the work.
He aligns well with Zimmerman and Clark, as all three prioritize public safety and ensuring that the city delivers the public services that residents pay for. But he also offers many other ideas for programs big (using Portland Clean Energy dollars to establish a fareless transit system) and small (creating a “City Beer Hall” gathering venue that showcases Portland breweries and food vendors).
Electing Arnold, however, would require displacing an incumbent and he’s likely to appeal to the same voters as Zimmerman and Clark. While we believe he would be a strong addition to the City Council, we urge voters to rank him third.
Other candidates
We are not endorsing Councilor Mitch Green, but the reasons go beyond any one policy or any one incident. Rather, it is his pattern of poor judgment.
That starts with his push to ban the sale of foie gras in Portland. As we noted above, the ban affects only about a dozen businesses within city limits, yet councilors spent multiple meetings taking public comment and discussing the ordinance, even as they grappled with budget cuts. The ordinance should have died after a Council committee voted it down, but Green tapped his allies to bring it to the full Council. The effort showed an excessive investment of elected officials’ time for an ordinance of little consequence.
Then there’s his failure to pay his property taxes for more than six months until after he was contacted by The Oregonian/OregonLive’s Shane Dixon Kavanaugh. That flub — a misunderstanding, he said — was followed up by his decision to start an illegal brush fire in his backyard in August as wildfires were already raging through the state.
There’s the collection of his old tweets from 2020 and 2021 that express his “pure hatred” for police, his contempt for business owners as “leeching off the community,” and his musing over the possibility of anti-vaccine police officers being fired, losing their benefits and then dying of COVID-19.
We’ve appreciated that he has acknowledged his mistakes and walked back the tenor of his tweets. But Portland needs leaders who aren’t just discovering respect for police officers and businesses — both of which are key to helping the city thrive.
Of the other eight candidates running for District 4, we interviewed Jamey Evenstar, 50, and capital projects manager Jeremy Smith, 29. As someone who worked for former Commissioner Chloe Eudaly and Councilor Candace Avalos, Evenstar has considerable experience in policy, although we have concerns about their impacts. That said, her stances are in line with many of Green’s positions, which could make her a suitable alternative for voters looking for one. We did not feel that Smith advanced anything more than standard democratic socialist ideas.
It may be too early to pass judgment on Portland’s new form of government, but it’s not too late to demand leaders who are more responsive, representative and committed to constituents. Voters in Districts 3 and 4 should do their part and elect people who fit that bill.
-The Oregonian/OregonLive Editorial Board
